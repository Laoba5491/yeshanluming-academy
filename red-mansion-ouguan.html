《紅樓夢》裡有許多女孩。有些人一出場，便光彩奪目，像林黛玉、薛寶釵、王熙鳳那樣，讓人過目難忘；可還有一些女孩，出場不多，名字很容易被人忘掉，但她們偶然說出的幾句話，卻像黑夜裡的一點燈火，忽然把整部《紅樓夢》所要說的那個「情」字照亮了。 藕官就是這樣的人。 她原本是賈府從蘇州買來的小戲子之一。那時元春省親，賈府為了撐場面，花重金從南方買來十二個學戲的小女孩。她們年紀都很小，許多人還只是孩子，便被迫離鄉背井，學唱、學走、學哭、學笑。 從進入戲班的那一天開始，她們的人生便不再屬於自己。 藕官在戲裡演的是小生。戲台上的小生，多半是風流才子，因此她常與演小旦的菂官搭戲。兩人在台上演夫妻，演得久了，感情深了，竟連台下也不願分開。 曹雪芹這樣寫，並不是批判這些小戲子胡鬧，而是在寫一種真正的「情」。她們年紀還小，未必懂得什麼男女之欲，可她們彼此依戀、彼此需要，那種感情，其實已經比許多成年人的婚姻更加真誠。 可惜，《紅樓夢》的世界，就是容不下真情。後來，菂官病死了。 一個小戲子死去，在賈府裡算不得什麼大事。那些管事婆子和主子們，大概連她長什麼樣都未必記得。可是對藕官來說，卻像天塌了一樣。 戲台上的那個人沒有了，生活中的那個人也沒有了。從此以後，再也沒有人陪她一起上台，也沒有人與她起居坐臥、片刻不離。

結果，她被守園子的婆子當場抓住。婆子大聲斥責她，還揚言要報告給王熙鳳等主子，嚴加懲罰。在大觀園裡私自燒紙，是嚴重違反規矩的事情。藕官只是一個身份低微的小戲子，如果真的被鬧到王熙鳳等人面前，後果可想而知。 恰巧賈寶玉從那裡經過。為了保護藕官，他先撒謊騙婆子說，是「林姑娘叫她來燒寫壞的廢字紙」；隨後又編造了一個故事，說自己夢見了「杏花神」，需要藕官幫忙燒紙除穢。 婆子見有寶玉撐腰，事情又牽涉到林黛玉，便不敢再追究，藕官才得以化險為夷。 婆子走後，寶玉向藕官詢問原委。藕官感謝寶玉好心為她解圍，也知道寶玉是與自己「一流的人物」，便含淚告訴他，這件事只有芳官和蕊官知道。 只是其中的隱情，她不便親口細說，便讓寶玉回去悄悄詢問芳官。寶玉回到怡紅院後，才從芳官口中得知藕官與菂官之間的故事。 為了更加準確地瞭解這些人小心大的小戲子究竟在想什麼，我們還是抄錄曹雪芹的原文。 「寶玉便使個眼色給芳官，芳官本來伶俐，又學了幾年戲，何事不知？便裝肚子疼，不吃飯了。襲人道：『既不吃，就在屋裏作伴兒。把粥留下，你餓了再吃。』說著去了。 寶玉便將方才見藕官，如何謊言護庇，如何藕官叫我問你，細細的告訴他一遍。又問：『他祭的到底是誰？』芳官聽了，眼圈兒一紅，又嘆一口氣，說道： 『這事說來，藕官兒也是胡鬧。』 寶玉忙問：『如何？』芳官道：『他祭的就是死了的菂官。』 寶玉道：『他們兩個也算朋友，也是應當的。』 芳官道：『哪裏又是什麼朋友？那都是傻想頭。他是小生，菂官是小旦，往常時他們扮作兩口兒，每日唱戲的時候，都裝著那麼親熱，一來二去，兩個人就裝糊塗了，倒像真的一樣兒。後來兩個竟是你疼我，我愛你。菂官兒一死，他就哭的死去活來的，到如今不忘，所以每節燒紙。後來補了蕊官，我們見他也是那樣，就問他：「為什麼得了新的就把舊的忘了？」他說：「不是忘了。比如人家男人死了女人，也有再娶的，只是不把死的丟開不提便是有情分了。」你說他是傻不是呢？』 寶玉聽了這獃話，獨合了他的獃性，不覺又喜又悲，又稱奇道絕。拉著芳官囑咐道：『既如此說，我有一句話囑咐你，須得你告訴他：以後斷不可燒紙，逢時按節，只備一爐香，一心誠虔就能感應了。我那案上也只設著一個爐，我有心事，不論日期時常焚香。隨便新水新茶就供一盞，或有鮮花鮮果，甚至葷腥素菜都可。只在敬心，不在虛名。以後快叫他不可再燒紙了！』芳官聽了，便答應著。」 藕官在戲中經常與菂官扮演夫妻，戲外也情同伴侶。菂官死後，她哭得死去活來，每逢節日，仍然偷偷祭奠菂官。 可是後來，戲班裡又讓蕊官接替菂官，與藕官搭戲。時間久了，藕官與蕊官也產生了感情。 其他小戲子便問藕官：「為什麼得了新的，就把舊的忘了？」 藕官回答：「不是忘了。比如人家男人死了女人，也有再娶的，只是不把死的丟開不提便是有情分了。」 這便是藕官最不同尋常的地方。 她沒有因為菂官死去，就假裝自己從此不會再愛；也沒有因為有了新的伴侶，就把死去的菂官忘掉。 在她看來，人是可以再愛的。重新愛上一個人，不等於背叛已經死去的人。只要心裡仍然記得曾經真正愛過的那個人，不把她徹底丟開，不假裝她從來沒有存在過，便仍然算是有情。 這種看似孩子氣的說法，卻令寶玉深感震撼，連聲稱奇道絕。 藕官年紀雖小，思維卻遠遠超過當時的許多公子小姐。她不像晴雯那樣鋒利，也不像齡官那樣冷傲，可她骨子裡卻有一種很特別的倔強。 她簡單的一句話：「只是不把死的丟開不提便是有情分了」，無意中構成了對封建禮教的挑戰。 那個時代要求女人從一而終。丈夫活著，女人必須依附丈夫；丈夫死了，女人也要終身守節。誰能夠為死去的丈夫守寡一生，誰就是值得旌表的「貞婦」「烈婦」。 至於男人，妻子死後再娶，卻是天經地義。 藕官正是借用了男人死去妻子以後可以續弦的道理，來解釋自己的感情。她未必有意識地要反抗封建禮教，可是她的這種想法，卻無意中戳穿了那個時代男女之間極不公平的道德標準。 她既不假裝順從「從一而終」的社會規矩，也不願意假裝無情無義、喜新忘舊。她承認人的感情會發生變化，人會重新愛上另一個人；可她也認為，真正愛過的人，不應該因為死去，就被徹底遺忘。 這並不是小孩子的胡言亂語，而是她對何謂真「情」的一種通透理解。 這種想法，在《紅樓夢》所描寫的那個時代，極為罕見，也極為超前。 因為那個時代的道德，常常只用兩種標準評判女人：一種是「貞婦」，守著一個死人過一輩子；另一種是「淫婦」，只要重新愛上別人，便會被視為薄情，甚至被認為不守婦道。 可是藕官偏偏不肯這樣想，她既承認自己的新感情，也不願背叛舊感情。這種理性與通透，甚至比許多成年人更加成熟。 她的這種不合時宜的超前思維， 最受震動的人，就是賈寶玉。 寶玉本來就是一個「情種」，他最不能忍受別人拿禮教和規矩壓人。當他聽完芳官轉述藕官的那些話後，非但沒有笑她荒唐，反而生出了一種忽逢知己般的深深感動。 因為藕官說的，正好合了他的「獃性」。 在寶玉眼裡，人若真有感情，便不應該完全被那些規矩束縛。喜歡就是喜歡，思念就是思念。死去的人不應該因為死了便被遺忘，活著的人也不應該因為曾經愛過一個人，就從此不許再愛，只能在孤獨中活受罪。 所以，寶玉不但沒有責怪藕官私自燒紙，反而替她遮掩，還特意勸她說，以後不必再冒險偷偷燒紙，只要心裡有情，焚一炷香，供一盞清茶，也就足夠了。 「只在敬心，不在虛名。」 這句話，不只是寶玉對藕官說的，也是曹雪芹借寶玉之口，對世俗禮法作出的回答。 真正的感情，不在於別人是否看見，不在於排場是否隆重，也不在於是否符合世俗規矩，而在於心裡是否真正記得。 這一段故事，園子幽深，杏花樹下，一個小戲子偷偷祭奠亡人，幾個孩子談論生死與愛情。它不合時宜，也不合那個社會的規矩，恍惚間竟像一段幽美而淒涼的《聊齋》故事。 可越美，就越讓人難受，因為她們都太脆弱了。 藕官後來與蕊官親近，旁人便譏笑她薄情。其實那些人根本不懂得何謂真正的「情」。 真正深情的人，往往反而更加明白：人活著，是不能只依靠回憶活下去的。 愛一個已經死去的人，並不意味著自己也必須停止生活；重新愛上一個活著的人，也不意味著從前的感情都是虛假。 藕官仍然記得菂官。清明節到了，她冒著受罰、甚至被趕出去的危險，躲在杏花樹下，偷偷為菂官燒紙。可是她也願意接受蕊官，願意讓自己仍然活在新的感情之中，這便是她最難得的地方。 後來，王夫人清查怡紅院，芳官、藕官、蕊官這些戲子出身的女孩，都被當作「狐狸精」一類的人物，趕出了大觀園。 她們的乾娘本來想把她們領回去，日後自行婚配。三個女孩卻都不肯，寧願削髮為尼。 芳官、蕊官跟隨水月庵的智通而去，藕官則跟隨地藏庵的圓信出家。 她們為什麼都選擇出家？ 大概因為她們知道，一旦回到乾娘手中，自己的婚姻和命運，仍然不會由自己作主。她們很可能會像商品一樣，再一次被安排、被處置，甚至被轉手賣掉。 她們出家，未必是因為真正看破紅塵，也未必是因為真正嚮往青燈古佛。那可能只是這些走投無路的女孩，為自己所能找到的最後一條出路。 可是，從戲班到大觀園，再從大觀園走入空門，她們看似換了幾個地方，實際上始終沒有得到真正的自由。 這些女孩，本來就是被命運遺棄的人。她們在戲台上唱盡才子佳人的悲歡離合，自己卻從來沒有真正掌握過人生。 她們在台上扮演帝王將相、才子佳人，說著最美麗的情話，演著最動人的愛情；可是下了戲台，她們卻連選擇自己要不要唱、要不要嫁人、要過什麼生活的權利都沒有。 而藕官，大概是她們中間少數真正懂得何為「情」的人。 她知道，一個人在所愛之人逝去以後，仍然可以為了活下去，再去愛另一個人。只要沒有遺忘真正愛過的人，就不算薄情。 她在戲台上扮演的是風流多情的小生，可她留給寶玉、也留給世人的，卻是比許多成年人更加通透的愛情道理：活著的人可以繼續去愛，死去的人也不應該被遺忘。 （未完待續）
There are many girls in Dream of the Red Chamber. Some shine brilliantly from the moment they appear—Lin Daiyu, Xue Baochai, and Wang Xifeng, for example—and remain unforgettable. Others appear only briefly, and even their names are easily forgotten. Yet a few words spoken by one of these girls can be like a small light in the darkness, suddenly illuminating the meaning of that one word—“love”—that lies at the heart of the entire novel. Ouguan was such a girl. She was one of the young opera performers purchased from Suzhou by the Jia family. When Yuanchun returned home for her imperial visit, the family spent a great deal of money bringing twelve young girls from the south to study opera, all to create a magnificent spectacle for the occasion. They were very young, many of them still children, yet they were forced to leave their homes and families and learn how to sing, how to move, how to weep, and how to smile. From the day they entered the opera troupe, their lives no longer belonged to them. Ouguan played young male roles onstage. Such characters were usually elegant and romantic scholars, so she was often paired with Diguan, who played young female roles. The two performed as husband and wife. After acting together for so long, their affection deepened until they became unwilling to be separated even when they were no longer onstage. Cao Xueqin was not condemning these young performers for behaving foolishly. He was writing about something genuine: love. They were still young and might not yet have understood physical desire between men and women, but they depended upon and needed each other. The affection between them was more sincere than many marriages between adults. Unfortunately, the world of Dream of the Red Chamber had no room for true love. Later, Diguan fell ill and died. The death of one young opera performer was hardly a significant event in the Jia household. The supervisory matrons and masters probably could not even remember what she had looked like. To Ouguan, however, it was as though the sky itself had collapsed. The person who had stood beside her onstage was gone, and so was the person who had shared her life away from the stage. Never again would anyone perform at her side or remain with her day and night, unwilling to be separated from her even for a moment.

During the Qingming Festival, Ouguan—who had by then been assigned to serve Lin Daiyu—hid in a secluded corner of the Grand Prospect Garden and burned paper offerings for her deceased stage partner, Diguan, the person she had truly loved. A matron guarding the garden caught her in the act. The woman loudly berated her and threatened to report her to Wang Xifeng and the other masters so that she would be severely punished. Secretly burning paper offerings in the Grand Prospect Garden was a serious violation of its rules. Ouguan was merely a lowly young opera performer. If the matter were truly brought before Wang Xifeng and the others, the consequences were easy to imagine. By coincidence, Jia Baoyu happened to pass by. To protect Ouguan, he first lied to the matron, claiming that “Miss Lin had ordered her to burn some discarded sheets of paper on which mistakes had been written.” He then invented another story, saying that he had dreamed of the “Spirit of the Apricot Blossom” and needed Ouguan to burn paper offerings to dispel some ritual impurity. Seeing that Baoyu was protecting Ouguan, and that the matter now also involved Lin Daiyu, the matron did not dare pursue it any further. Ouguan thus escaped a dangerous situation unharmed. After the matron left, Baoyu asked Ouguan what had happened. Grateful that he had rescued her, Ouguan also recognized him as “someone of the same kind” as herself. With tears in her eyes, she told him that only Fangguan and Ruiguan knew about the matter. Yet the secret was too intimate for her to explain in person, so she asked Baoyu to return home and question Fangguan privately. Only after Baoyu returned to Happy Red Court and spoke with Fangguan did he learn the story of Ouguan and Diguan. To understand more accurately what these young performers—children in age, yet adults in thought—truly believed, let us return to Cao Xueqin’s original text. “Baoyu gave Fangguan a meaningful look. Fangguan was naturally quick-witted, and after several years of studying opera, there was little she failed to understand. She therefore pretended to have a stomachache and said she did not want to eat. Xiren said, ‘If you are not eating, stay here and keep him company. I shall leave the congee for you, and you can have it when you are hungry.’ With that, she left. “Baoyu then told Fangguan in detail how he had encountered Ouguan, how he had protected her with a lie, and how Ouguan had asked him to question Fangguan. He then asked, ‘For whom was she making those offerings?’ Fangguan’s eyes reddened. She sighed and said: “‘When you hear the story, you will agree that Ouguan was behaving foolishly.’ “Baoyu hurriedly asked, ‘How so?’ Fangguan replied, ‘The person she was honoring was Diguan, who died.’ “Baoyu said, ‘The two of them were friends, so it was only right that she should honor her.’ “Fangguan replied, ‘Friends? It was nothing so simple. It was all a foolish fantasy. Ouguan played young male roles, and Diguan played young female roles. They used to perform as husband and wife. Every day when they acted, they pretended to be so affectionate that, as time passed, the two became confused and began to behave as though it were real. Eventually, one cherished the other, and the other loved her in return. When Diguan died, Ouguan wept until she was more dead than alive. She has never forgotten her and still burns paper offerings at every festival. Later, Ruiguan replaced Diguan. When we saw Ouguan treating her in the same way, we asked, “Why did you forget the old one as soon as you found someone new?” She replied, “I have not forgotten. When a man’s wife dies, he may marry again. As long as he does not cast the dead woman aside and never speak of her, he still possesses true feeling.” Would you not say that she is foolish?’ “Hearing these foolish words, which so perfectly accorded with his own foolish nature, Baoyu felt both joy and sorrow. He marveled at them and praised them without end. Taking Fangguan by the hand, he instructed her, ‘Since that is the case, I have something I want you to tell her. She must never burn paper offerings again. Whenever a festival comes, she need only prepare a single burner of incense. If her heart is sincere and reverent, it will be enough to reach the spirit. I too keep only one incense burner on my table. Whenever something weighs upon my heart, I burn incense, regardless of the date. A cup of fresh water or tea may be offered, or fresh flowers and fruit—even meat, fish, or vegetarian food will do. What matters is the reverence in one’s heart, not empty outward forms. Tell her that from now on, she must never burn paper offerings again!’ Fangguan promised that she would.” Ouguan often played husband and wife with Diguan onstage, while away from the stage they were companions in love. After Diguan died, Ouguan wept until she was more dead than alive. Whenever a festival arrived, she still secretly made offerings to her. Later, however, the opera troupe assigned Ruiguan to replace Diguan and perform opposite Ouguan. As time passed, affection also developed between Ouguan and Ruiguan. The other young performers therefore asked Ouguan, “Why did you forget the old one as soon as you found someone new?” Ouguan replied, “I have not forgotten. When a man’s wife dies, he may marry again. As long as he does not cast the dead woman aside and never speak of her, he still possesses true feeling.” This was what made Ouguan so extraordinary. She did not pretend that, because Diguan had died, she would never love again. Nor did she forget the dead Diguan simply because she had found a new companion. In Ouguan’s eyes, a person could love again. Falling in love with someone new did not mean betraying the one who had died. As long as she still remembered the person she had once truly loved—as long as she did not cast her aside completely or pretend that she had never existed—she remained faithful in feeling. These seemingly childish words profoundly shook Baoyu, filling him with wonder and admiration. Although Ouguan was very young, her thinking far surpassed that of many young masters and ladies of her time. She was not as sharp as Qingwen, nor as cold and proud as Lingguan, but deep within her was a distinctive kind of stubbornness. Her simple statement—“As long as he does not cast the dead woman aside and never speak of her, he still possesses true feeling”—unintentionally became a challenge to feudal morality. The society of that time demanded lifelong fidelity from women. While her husband lived, a woman had to depend upon him; after he died, she was expected to preserve her chastity for the rest of her life. A woman who remained a widow until death was praised and officially honored as a “chaste wife” or a “virtuous widow.” For a man, however, marrying again after his wife’s death was regarded as entirely natural. Ouguan borrowed precisely this principle—that a man could take another wife after the first had died—to explain her own feelings. She might not consciously have intended to rebel against feudal morality, yet her idea inadvertently exposed the profoundly unequal moral standards imposed upon men and women in that age. She neither pretended to obey society’s demand for lifelong fidelity to one person, nor wished to appear heartless and faithless by abandoning the old for the new. She acknowledged that human feelings could change and that a person could fall in love again. Yet she also believed that someone who had once been truly loved should not be completely forgotten merely because she had died. This was not the childish nonsense of an ignorant girl. It was a clear and penetrating understanding of what true love meant. Such an idea was extraordinarily rare and far ahead of its time in the society portrayed in Dream of the Red Chamber. The morality of that age frequently judged women according to only two standards. One was the “chaste woman,” who spent her entire life faithful to a dead man. The other was the “wanton woman,” who, if she ever loved again, would be condemned as faithless and accused of violating womanly virtue. Ouguan refused to think in this way. She acknowledged her new love but was equally unwilling to betray the old. Her reason and clarity were more mature than those of many adults. The person most deeply shaken by her untimely and advanced way of thinking was Jia Baoyu. Baoyu had always been a lover by nature, and nothing distressed him more than seeing people oppressed by ritual morality and social rules. When he heard Fangguan recount Ouguan’s words, he did not laugh at her as absurd. Instead, he was deeply moved, as though he had suddenly encountered a kindred spirit. For what Ouguan said perfectly accorded with Baoyu’s own “foolish nature.” In Baoyu’s eyes, if people truly possessed feeling, they should not be completely bound by rules. Love was love, and longing was longing. The dead should not be forgotten merely because they had died; nor should the living, simply because they had once loved someone, be forbidden ever to love again and condemned to suffer in loneliness. Baoyu therefore did not blame Ouguan for secretly burning paper offerings. Instead, he concealed what she had done and advised her that she need not risk doing so again. If love remained in her heart, then burning a single stick of incense and offering one cup of clear tea would be enough. “What matters is the reverence in one’s heart, not empty outward forms.” These words were not merely Baoyu’s advice to Ouguan. They were also Cao Xueqin’s answer to conventional ritual and morality, spoken through Baoyu. True love does not depend upon whether others can see it, whether the ceremony is magnificent, or whether it conforms to society’s rules. What matters is whether the person is truly remembered in one’s heart. The story unfolded deep within the garden, beneath an apricot tree: a young opera performer secretly honoring the dead while several children discussed love, death, and life. It belonged neither to its time nor to the rules of that society. In its strange beauty and sorrow, it seemed almost like a tale from Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio. Yet the more beautiful it became, the more painful it felt, because these girls were so terribly vulnerable. When Ouguan later became close to Ruiguan, others mocked her as faithless. In truth, those people did not understand what genuine love meant. Those who love most deeply often understand something that others do not: the living cannot survive on memories alone. Loving someone who has died does not mean that one must also cease living. Falling in love with someone who is still alive does not mean that all former love was false. Ouguan still remembered Diguan. When the Qingming Festival arrived, she risked punishment and even expulsion from the garden to hide beneath an apricot tree and secretly burn paper offerings for her. Yet she was also willing to accept Ruiguan and allow herself to continue living within a new love. This was what made her most remarkable. Later, when Lady Wang purged Happy Red Court, Fangguan, Ouguan, Ruiguan, and the other girls who had come from the opera troupe were treated as if they were “little fox spirits” and driven from the Grand Prospect Garden. Their foster mothers wanted to take them back and later arrange marriages for them as they saw fit. All three girls refused and said they would rather shave their heads and become nuns. Fangguan and Ruiguan left with Zhitong of Water Moon Convent, while Ouguan entered religious life under Yuanxin of Ksitigarbha Convent. Why did all three choose the religious life? Probably because they understood that once they returned to the hands of their foster mothers, neither their marriages nor their futures would belong to them. Like merchandise, they would very likely be arranged, disposed of, or even sold once again. They did not necessarily become nuns because they had truly renounced the mortal world, nor because they sincerely yearned for oil lamps, Buddhist images, and a life of religious devotion. For these desperate girls with nowhere else to go, it may simply have been the last path still open to them. From the opera troupe to the Grand Prospect Garden, and from the Grand Prospect Garden into religious life, they appeared to move from one place to another, yet they never obtained true freedom. These girls had been abandoned by fate from the beginning. Onstage, they performed every joy and sorrow of gifted scholars and beautiful women, yet they never possessed the power to direct their own lives. Before an audience, they played emperors, generals, scholars, and beauties. They spoke the most beautiful words of love and performed the most moving romances. Once they stepped off the stage, however, they did not even possess the right to decide whether they wished to sing, whether they wished to marry, or what kind of life they wanted to live. Among them, Ouguan was probably one of the very few who truly understood the meaning of love. She knew that after the death of someone dearly loved, a person could still choose to live and to love another. As long as the person once truly loved was not forgotten, loving again was not a betrayal. Onstage, Ouguan played a charming and romantic young man. What she left to Baoyu—and to all who came after her—was an understanding of love more lucid than that possessed by many adults: the living may continue to love, and the dead should never be forgotten.
